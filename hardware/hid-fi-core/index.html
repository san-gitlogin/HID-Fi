One honest trade-off. PLA is fine for the fit coupon, the peg gauge or a desk test. For a unit left running for days, use PETG: PLA softens near 55 °C, and the pegs would loosen.

How the base clips in, with no screws

Closing: hook the plate's front tongue into the slot inside the front wall, then press the back down with your thumb until it sits flush. Two pegs on the plate slide up into two sockets moulded in the top shell's back corners.

Why it stays put: each peg has three thin ribs that squash slightly as it goes in, like a wall plug. That gives a firm grip even if your printer prints a little over or under size. In a 1 m drop onto its top, the plate needs only about 4 N of grip per peg to stay on; a snug press fit holds more than that (estimated; the peg gauge lets you feel it).

Opening: put a coin in the small notch at the back edge of the plate and lever gently. The pegs slide out, then unhook the tongue.

Tune it once. Print the peg gauge first: three pegs and three holes at three tightnesses. Press each pair together and pick the one that is snug but still comes out with a fingernail. The files ship with pair 2's grip. If pair 1 or 3 suits your printer better, open an issue on GitHub with the pair number and the parts can be regenerated with that grip.

Print it

PartMaterialOn the bedSettings

On your own printer

  • Use the files from Download: they are already turned the way they sit on the bed, so do not rotate them.
  • Print in this order: fit coupon, peg gauge, unibody, plate, light pipe (optional).
  • The unibody prints top face down. On a textured sheet the top comes out with a fine matte texture and no layer lines.
  • 0.12 mm layers for the unibody (its walls are what people see), 0.16–0.2 mm for the rest. 100% cooling for PETG after the first layers, for the vent slopes.
  • Elephant-foot compensation 0.15 mm keeps the vents and lettering sharp.
  • No supports anywhere.

At a print shop or online service

  • Shops charge a minimum per file (often ₹100), not per gram. Cheapest: upload the single kit-all.stl (every black part: unibody, plate, trim guide, fit coupon, peg gauge; about 18 g). Or, to check the fit before printing the shell, upload the two kit files instead of separate parts: kit-test.stl (fit coupon + peg gauge) first, then kit-core.stl (unibody + plate). Two minimums instead of seven.
  • The light pipe must be clear PETG ("transparent" or "natural"). Black blocks the light. Order it separately in clear, or skip it and use the free hot-glue window.
  • Then send unibody.stl and plate.stl from the zip, with PRINT-NOTES.txt: "PETG, no supports, print exactly as oriented in the files, unibody at 0.12 mm layers."
  • Ask for photos before they ship, so you can check that the vents and lettering came out.
  • Skip the light pipe at a shop: it needs clear material, which means another file and another minimum charge. The hot-glue window costs nothing and glows the same.

Fit check, before the full print

  1. Print fit-coupon.stl: the first 14 mm of the shell, the end with the USB-C windows. About 15 minutes.
  2. Hold it face down and slide the board's two sockets into the windows from inside. The board edge should touch the inside of the front wall, with the sockets sitting in the windows.
  3. Plug in your thickest USB-C cable from outside. It must click fully home, the same as on the bare board.
  4. Print the peg gauge and press pegs 1, 2 and 3 into holes 1, 2 and 3. Pick the pair that is snug but comes out with a fingernail. The default is 2.
  5. If the cable stops short, measure how far the sockets stick out past the board edge (the design assumes 0.6 mm) and open an issue on GitHub with the number: the parts are regenerated from it and every check reruns. Otherwise, print the real parts.

Assemble

  1. Pins. If your board has pins soldered underneath, they must go; the shell has no room for them. Switch on "Pins as shipped" in the model for the trim method, and use the printable trim guide. Boards without pins skip this.
  2. Light. Press the printed light pipe in from inside, or fill the 2 mm window from inside with a drop of clear hot glue and let it set.
  3. Board. Unibody face down. Lower the board in components first, sockets into their windows, then slide it forward until it stops (about 0.8 mm).
  4. Plate. Hook its front tongue into the slot inside the front wall, then press the back down with your thumb until it sits flush: the two pegs slide into their sockets.
  5. Done. Plug USB into the computer, COM if you need serial.

Taking it apart

A coin in the notch at the back edge of the plate, lever gently, then unhook the tongue. Tilt the board back 0.8 mm and lift it out. Nothing is glued or screwed, so you can reuse the shell for every board you test.

Your settings stay

The shell changes nothing in software: your board keeps its shortcuts, macros and networks.

Design review: what I checked, found and fixed

Found and fixed in this review

  • The inside of the top shell was missing from the print files. A build-order mistake cut away every internal feature: the screw posts, the two clamp ribs that hold the board, the status-light cell, the LED blackout pod and the pinhole guides. The interference check could not see it, because missing plastic never collides. You asked where the screws go, and that question exposed it. Fixed, and a new check now fails the build if any added feature is cut away. I proved it fails on the old code.
  • The Sense and Go bases had the same bug (bay walls, columns, mic ribs). Restoring them revealed five real clashes, all now fixed: the mic boards rubbing their ribs, two alignment pins under the Go's switch and boost converter, and the Go door screw longer than its hole.
  • Light pipe: a 1.94 mm pipe in a 2.0 mm hole would not fit a printed hole, so it is now 1.9 in 2.1. Its flange moved to the bottom end so it prints without hanging in the air.
  • Clearances widened for printing: the base plate in its pocket from 0.15 to 0.25 mm, and the front tongue in its slot from 0.05 to 0.15 mm. The tongue's underside is now sloped so it prints cleanly.
  • Screws removed: press-fit ribbed pegs and a coin notch instead, with a gauge print to set the grip.
  • Print-ready files: every part is now exported already turned the way it sits on the bed. A print shop given the old files could have printed the shell upside down with supports.
